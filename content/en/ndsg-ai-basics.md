The revised Federal Act on Data Protection (Bundesgesetz über den Datenschutz, DSG, often called the "nDSG") has been in force since September 1, 2023. The Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (Eidgenössischer Datenschutz- und Öffentlichkeitsbeauftragter, EDÖB) has made clear: the DSG is **technology-neutral** and therefore **directly applicable to AI-driven data processing**. A dedicated Swiss AI act is not required for this purpose.

This page summarizes the key points for Swiss SMEs. It does not replace legal advice.

## Who is affected?

The EDÖB addresses **manufacturers, providers (Anbieter), and users** of AI applications. Anyone who processes personal data (Personendaten) using AI — whether internally or via a cloud service — remains responsible under data protection law and must comply with the applicable legal obligations.

## Transparency and information {#transparency}

The purpose, functioning, and data sources of AI-driven processing must be **disclosed transparently**. For language models that communicate directly with users, those users have the right to know:

- whether and to what extent they are corresponding with a machine;
- whether the data they enter is reused to improve self-learning programs or for other purposes.

Applications capable of manipulating the faces, images, or voice messages of identifiable persons must also be clearly identifiable as such.

## Objection and human review {#human-review}

The right to transparency is closely linked to the right to **object** to automated data processing or to request that **automated individual decisions be reviewed by a human**.

## High risks and data protection impact assessment {#dpia}

AI-driven processing involving high risks is permissible in principle, but requires **appropriate safeguards**. Where high risks are present, the DSG requires a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung, DSFA)**.

Applications aimed at undermining privacy and informational self-determination — such as blanket real-time facial recognition or "social scoring" — are **prohibited** under data protection law.

## Who within the company is responsible {#responsibility}

Responsibility under data protection law lies with the company itself (the "controller" within the meaning of the DSG), not with the AI tool or its provider. The DSG does not require companies to appoint a dedicated AI officer. Private companies may appoint a data protection advisor (Art. 10 DSG), but are not obliged to do so.

In practice, however, a clearly designated person is still needed, otherwise questions and decisions go unaddressed. It has proven effective to name a specific person who:

- keeps the inventory of AI tools in use up to date;
- decides on new tools or prepares such decisions;
- serves as the point of contact for questions and incidents;
- organizes training and keeps the internal AI policy current.

In small companies, this is usually the owner or the management. What matters is that the task is explicitly assigned and that time is set aside for it. Institutions supervised by FINMA are subject to further-reaching expectations regarding governance and responsibilities (see [FINMA expectations for AI governance](/de/finma-ai-expectations/)).

## What SMEs can do now {#next-steps}

1. Inventory: Which AI tools process personal data?
2. Update transparency notices and internal policies.
3. Plan a DPIA where high risk is present.
4. Clarify with providers: data processing agreements, use for training, storage location.
5. Ensure human review for automated decisions with significant effects.
6. Designate a person responsible for the company's use of AI (see [Who within the company is responsible](#responsibility)).
