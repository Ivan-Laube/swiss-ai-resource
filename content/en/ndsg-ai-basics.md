The revised Federal Act on Data Protection (Bundesgesetz über den Datenschutz, DSG, often called the "nFADP") has been in force since 1 September 2023. The Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (Eidgenössischer Datenschutz- und Öffentlichkeitsbeauftragter, FDPIC) makes clear that the DSG is **technology-neutral** and therefore **directly applicable to AI-driven data processing**. A separate Swiss AI law is not required for this purpose.

This page summarizes the key points for Swiss SMEs. It does not replace legal advice.

## Who is affected?

The FDPIC's guidance is addressed to **manufacturers, providers (Anbieter), and users** of AI applications. Anyone who processes personal data (Personendaten) using AI — whether internally or via a cloud service — remains responsible under data protection law and must comply with the applicable statutory obligations.

## Transparency and information {#transparency}

The purpose, functioning, and data sources of AI-driven processing must be disclosed **transparently**. In the case of language models that communicate directly with users, those users have the right to know:

- whether and to what extent they are corresponding with a machine;
- whether data they enter is reused to improve self-learning programs or for other purposes.

Applications capable of manipulating the faces, images, or voice recordings of identifiable individuals must also be clearly recognizable as such.

## Objection and human review {#human-review}

The right to transparency is closely linked to the right to **object** to automated data processing or to demand that **automated individual decisions be reviewed by a human**.

## High risks and data protection impact assessment {#dpia}

AI-driven processing involving high risks is permissible in principle, but requires **appropriate safeguards**. Where high risks exist, the DSG requires a **data protection impact assessment (Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung, DPIA)**.

Applications aimed at undermining privacy and informational self-determination — such as blanket real-time facial recognition or "social scoring" — are **prohibited** under data protection law.

## Who is responsible within the company {#responsibility}

Responsibility under data protection law lies with the company itself (the "controller" within the meaning of the DSG), not with the AI tool or its provider. The DSG does not require companies to appoint a dedicated AI officer. Private companies may appoint a data protection advisor (Art. 10 DSG), but are not obligated to do so.

In practice, however, a clearly designated person is still needed; otherwise, questions and decisions tend to go unaddressed. It has proven effective to name a specific person who:

- keeps the inventory of AI tools in use up to date;
- decides on new tools or prepares such decisions;
- serves as the point of contact for questions and incidents;
- organizes training and keeps the internal AI policy up to date.

In small companies, this is usually the owner or the management. What matters is that the task is explicitly assigned and that some time is allocated for it. Institutions supervised by FINMA are subject to more extensive expectations regarding governance and responsibilities (see [FINMA expectations on AI governance](/de/finma-ai-expectations/)).

## What SMEs can do now {#next-steps}

1. Inventory: which AI tools process personal data?
2. Adapt transparency notices and internal policies. The [AI policy template (Word)](download:ai-policy-template) can serve as a starting point.
3. Plan a DPIA where high risk is present.
4. Clarify with providers: data processing agreements, use of data for training, storage location.
5. Ensure human review for automated decisions with significant effects.
6. Designate a person responsible for the use of AI (see [Who is responsible within the company](#responsibility)).
